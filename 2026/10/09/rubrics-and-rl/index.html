Rubric-Based Rewards and Reasoning Capacity

A rubric is a structured set of criteria that specifies the requirements for a high-quality response. These criteria may address instruction adherence, constraint satisfaction, the validity of intermediate reasoning, and final-answer correctness. By decomposing response quality into explicit evaluation targets, rubrics support more fine-grained assessment than a single holistic judgment.

In LLM post-training, rubric-based evaluations can serve as reward signals. An evaluator scores policy-generated responses against individual criteria, and the resulting judgments are aggregated into rewards for reinforcement learning. Such feedback can distinguish responses that receive the same outcome-based correctness score. However, greater detail in the evaluation criteria does not necessarily translate into more informative supervision. This raises a central question: can rubric-based rewards extend a model's reasoning capacity, or do they primarily improve the likelihood of generating solutions already accessible to the model?

Reasoning Capacity Beyond the Base Model

Yue et al. (2025) examine this distinction in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). They use pass@k across a range of sampling budgets, including large k, to compare RLVR-trained models with their base models. In the settings studied, RLVR frequently improves performance at small k, whereas base models can outperform their RLVR-trained counterparts at larger k. The authors interpret these findings as evidence that existing RLVR methods primarily improve sampling efficiency rather than introduce fundamentally new reasoning capabilities.

Pass@k as an Evaluation Metric

Pass@k measures the probability of obtaining at least one correct response among k sampled responses, averaged over evaluation problems. For example, if 60 out of 100 problems have at least one correct response within 16 samples each, the observed pass@16 is 60%. The metric assesses the availability of a correct solution within the sample set; it does not assess whether the model can select that solution.

Experimental Setup

We follow the SimpleRLZoo-RL experimental setup and adopt the pass@k evaluation approach used by Yue et al. (2025). We study a Qwen2.5-7B policy on four mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME24, MATH500, Minerva, and Olympiad. We evaluate pass@k across increasing sampling budgets, up to k = 1,024 on AIME24 and k = 128 on the other three benchmarks.

We investigate the effects of different rubric generators, judges, and reward designs on post-training performance. We further examine whether the benefits of rubric-based supervision arise from the rubric itself or from access to a stronger model. To investigate this distinction, we compare rubric-based training with conventional distillation and on-policy distillation (OPD), using the same underlying model to provide supervision in each comparison.

The judge is part of the learning signal

In this experiment, we keep the rubric generator fixed and vary the judge model. Rubrics are generated through an API using the model recorded in our experiment notes as qwen3.7-max. We then use two separately deployed judges, Qwen2.5-72B and Llama3.3-70B, to evaluate policy responses against these rubrics and provide reward signals for training.

The first result is easy to overlook: changing the judge changes what rubric-based RL learns. With the Qwen2.5-72B judge, the rubric curve is relatively close to the reference RL curve on AIME24. With the Llama3.3-70B judge, it falls visibly behind at larger sampling budgets. MATH500 is much less sensitive in these plots, while Minerva retains a substantial high-budget advantage for Base.

This supports judge dependence. A plausible explanation is that a judge's interpretation of the criteria changes which rollouts receive higher rewards. Because the rubric generator and judge are different models in this setup, we next investigate whether the observed differences reflect the judge model's preferences when interpreting and applying the generated rubrics.

Does using the same model for both roles help?

We use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B for both rubric generation and judging, with encouraging results: this configuration outperforms both Base and reference RL on AIME24, MATH500, and Olympiad. Yet Minerva still has lower high-budget coverage than Base. The result is promising on some tasks, not a universal recovery of coverage.

My interpretation is that agreement between the generator and judge may help make criteria actionable.

Correctness plus a rubric is not a guaranteed win

To investigate whether reward design affects the results, we introduce an alternative reward formulation that keeps correctness as the anchor and uses the rubric as an auxiliary signal. We test the following form:

reward = exact_correctness + 0.1 * rubric_score

If exact correctness is binary and the rubric score is normalized to [0, 1], a correct answer always receives a higher total reward than an incorrect one. That is a useful scoring property. It does not guarantee that policy optimization will improve every benchmark or preserve every rare solution.

For the Qwen3.5-35B-A3B configuration, the hybrid reward underperforms the rubric-only reward on AIME24, MATH500, and Olympiad. On Minerva, the hybrid reward improves over rubric-only training, but its high-budget coverage remains below Base.

To examine whether this finding holds with a different model, we use Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct for both rubric generation and judging. The results again show that the hybrid reward does not consistently improve over rubric-only training, with substantially lower coverage on AIME24 at large k. This suggests that adding an apparently sensible auxiliary reward can still change exploration or credit assignment in an unfavorable way.

The preceding experiments suggest that rubric-based rewards can expand the reasoning capability frontier of the RL-trained policy on some benchmarks. This raises a further question: do these gains arise from the rubric-based learning signal itself, or from the capabilities of a stronger rubric generator model? We investigate this question through two sets of comparisons. First, we compare rubric-based training with conventional distillation through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and with on-policy distillation (OPD), using the same underlying supervisory model within each comparison. Second, we use the policy model itself for both rubric generation and judging, allowing these roles to evolve alongside the policy during training.

Rubrics, distillation, and self-generated feedback

We first compare rubric-based RL with conventional distillation through supervised fine-tuning (SFT). In this baseline, a teacher model generates responses to the training problems, and the student model is fine-tuned on these teacher-generated responses. We use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B as the teacher for SFT and use the same model as both the rubric generator and judge in the rubric-based RL comparison. The results are shown below.

The traditional-distillation comparison is task-dependent. On AIME24, rubric-only RL is visibly ahead of the distillation run. On Minerva, distillation ends above rubric-only and slightly above Base. These curves argue against a single ranking of “rubrics versus distillation.” They also need matched training and teacher-compute budgets before supporting an efficiency claim.

Self-generated rubrics ask an especially interesting question: can a policy improve the feedback it gives itself? The online self-rubric experiment shows encouraging MATH500 and Olympiad trends. On AIME24 and Minerva, however, it does not exceed Base at the largest displayed budgets. This is evidence of mixed practical benefit, not proof that self-feedback generally creates new reasoning abilities.

We also compare rubric-based training with on-policy distillation (OPD). A rubric describes what to value; token-level distillation teaches a distribution over continuations. In OPD, the teacher provides token-level supervision on student-generated trajectories. The experiment below uses a Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct teacher.

The OPD run offers a useful reference. Despite access to a larger teacher, its AIME24 and Minerva curves remain below Base at large k. A stronger teacher is therefore not, by itself, enough to guarantee a broader observed solution set in these runs.

There is also a practical distinction worth testing: text rubrics can be reused across rollouts and obtained from text-only APIs, whereas logit-based OPD requires access to teacher distributions. That suggests a potential cost advantage for rubrics, but these experiments do not yet measure it. Response-based distillation is another option when logits are unavailable.

My current view is that rubrics are a way to design supervision, not an automatic capability amplifier. Their value depends on whether the criteria produce reliable, discriminative feedback on the policy's own rollouts. A higher reward or a higher pass@1 is useful, but it answers a narrower question than expanding coverage.

The experiments that would change my mind

  1. Measure gains and losses per problem. Compare Base-only, trained-only, shared, and unsolved sets under matched decoding and token budgets. Repeat sampling and report uncertainty; do not treat finite-sample failures as impossible problems.
  2. Inspect the reward signal inside each group. Log tie rates, reward dispersion, agreement with exact correctness, and rankings of partially correct solutions. Test whether rubric scores reward useful reasoning or merely presentation.
  3. Separate rubric quality from judge quality. Cross generator and judge models. Compare static, refreshed, and self-generated rubrics with identical scoring instructions, data, and training budgets.
  4. Run a clean reward ablation. Keep the rubric version fixed, normalize the score, vary its weight, and compare correctness-only, rubric-only, and hybrid rewards across seeds and checkpoints.
  5. Compare transfer and cost fairly. Match student compute and account for teacher tokens or API cost when comparing rubrics, distillation, and OPD. Evaluate on held-out tasks to check whether feedback transfers beyond the training criteria.
  6. Move to real agent tasks. Test tool-use decisions, execution constraints, and final delivery separately. Dynamic rubrics, a feedback buffer, curriculum learning, and comparisons with SDPO remain future experiments in these notes.
THE QUESTION I AM TAKING FORWARD

Can we make rubric feedback specific enough to guide learning, while preserving the rare solutions that broad exploration can still uncover?
