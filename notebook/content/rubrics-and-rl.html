01 / What is a rubric, and what can it teach a model?

Think of a rubric as a checklist for what makes an answer good. Did it follow the instructions? Did it account for the key constraints? Are the steps justified, and is the final answer correct? A rubric spells out these criteria so that a judge has something concrete to evaluate.

In LLM post-training, we can turn these judgments into rewards: a judge checks a response against the rubric, and the resulting scores become feedback for RL. This sounds useful. Two answers might both be wrong, but one could still get much of the reasoning right. A rubric gives us a way to tell them apart. But does more detailed feedback actually give the model more to learn from? Could it help the model solve problems it rarely, or never, solved before?

Does RL help a model go beyond what it already knows?

That question connects to Yue et al.'s work on reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). They ask whether RL develops reasoning beyond the base model or makes existing solutions easier to sample. To investigate this, they use pass@k across a range of sampling budgets, including large k. In their experiments, RLVR models often do better with few attempts, but base models can catch up or do better when given many attempts. Their interpretation is that RLVR mainly improves sampling efficiency in those settings. It does not settle what every possible RL method could learn.

So, what is pass@k?

Give a model k attempts at a problem. If at least one answer is correct, that problem counts as solved. Average this success across problems and sampling runs, and you get pass@k. For example, suppose we try 100 problems with 16 responses per problem. If 60 problems have at least one correct response, the observed pass@16 is 60%. We are checking whether a correct answer appears, not whether the model knows which answer to pick.

PASS@1

Can it get it right in one try?

A higher score means a single sampled answer is more likely to be correct.

PASS@K AT LARGE K

What if we let it try more times?

More attempts can uncover successful answers that are rare and easy to miss in a single try.

For a fair comparison, we need comparable sampling settings and budgets. Even then, failing within a fixed number of attempts does not mean a model could never solve the problem.

This is the question I bring to rubric-based rewards: do they help the model get to a correct answer faster, or help it reach more problems when we give it room to try? To explore that, I look at the whole pass@k curve.

03 / The judge is part of the learning signal

The first result is easy to overlook: changing the judge changes what rubric-based RL learns. With the Qwen2.5-72B judge, the rubric curve is relatively close to the reference RL curve on AIME24. With the Llama3.3-70B judge, it falls visibly behind at larger sampling budgets. MATH500 is much less sensitive in these plots, while Minerva retains a substantial high-budget advantage for Base.

{{FIG:judge-comparison.png|1280|576|Figure 1. Fixed API rubric generator, two judges. Top: Qwen2.5-72B. Bottom: Llama3.3-70B. AIME24 separates strongly at large k; the same separation is not uniform across benchmarks.|15}}

This supports judge dependence. It does not establish a proportional relationship between general judge capability and downstream performance: only two judge configurations are shown, and their independent judging accuracy is not measured here. A plausible explanation is that a judge's interpretation of the criteria changes which rollouts receive higher rewards.

Does using the same model for both roles help?

The next configuration uses Qwen3.5-35B-A3B for both rubric generation and judging. Its AIME24 curve ends above the displayed Base and reference RL curves, and MATH500 also shows a favorable trend. Yet Minerva still has lower high-budget coverage than Base. The result is promising on some tasks, not a universal recovery of coverage.

{{FIG:aligned-generator-judge.png|1516|358|Figure 2. Generator and judge both use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The favorable AIME24 trend coexists with a persistent Minerva coverage gap.|16}}

Interpretation, not isolation: agreement between the generator and judge may help make criteria actionable. But the model identity also changes relative to the first comparison. A clean test would cross generator and judge choices while holding prompts, training compute, data, and scoring rules fixed.

04 / Correctness plus a rubric is not a guaranteed win

A natural next step is to keep correctness as the anchor and use the rubric as an auxiliary signal. The notes test the following form:

reward = exact_correctness + 0.1 * rubric_score

If exact correctness is binary and the rubric score is normalized to [0, 1], a correct answer always receives a higher total reward than an incorrect one. That is a useful scoring property. It does not guarantee that policy optimization will improve every benchmark or preserve every rare solution.

{{FIG:hybrid-qwen35.png|1828|436|Figure 3. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B generator and judge. The rubric-only run is labeled legacy-v3; the hybrid run is labeled answer-free-v4. This comparison changes more than the reward formula.|16}}

For the Qwen3.5 configuration, the hybrid curve does not uniformly dominate the rubric-only curve. It is below rubric-only at the high-budget end of AIME24, while Minerva shows an improvement over rubric-only but remains below Base. Moreover, the figure changes the rubric version from legacy-v3 to answer-free-v4. We cannot attribute the difference solely to adding exact correctness.

{{FIG:hybrid-qwen25.png|1830|440|Figure 4. Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct generator and judge. The hybrid reward is notably weaker on AIME24 at large k; several other curves remain much closer.|16}}

The Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct configuration makes the trade-off clearer: the hybrid reward loses considerable high-budget AIME24 coverage. This is a useful negative result. Adding an apparently sensible auxiliary reward can still change exploration or credit assignment in an unfavorable way.

A mechanism to test. Group-relative optimization depends on informative differences between candidate rewards. Rubrics might introduce ties, inconsistent rankings, or rewards for properties weakly related to correctness. These are hypotheses: the plots alone do not measure them. A uniform rescaling of rewards is not sufficient evidence of “signal smoothing,” since normalization can largely cancel that scaling.

05 / Rubrics, distillation, and self-generated feedback

Rubrics and distillation convey teacher information through different channels. A rubric describes what to value; token-level distillation teaches a distribution over continuations. Neither channel is guaranteed to preserve or extend coverage in every setting.

{{FIG:distillation.png|1812|438|Figure 5. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B traditional distillation versus rubric-only RL. Distillation fares better at high-budget Minerva, while rubric RL is stronger on AIME24 in this comparison.|17}}

The traditional-distillation comparison is task-dependent. On AIME24, rubric-only RL is visibly ahead of the distillation run. On Minerva, distillation ends above rubric-only and slightly above Base. These curves argue against a single ranking of “rubrics versus distillation.” They also need matched training and teacher-compute budgets before supporting an efficiency claim.

{{FIG:self-rubric.png|1438|348|Figure 6. Online self-rubric RL with Qwen2.5-7B. Some benchmarks improve, but AIME24 and Minerva remain below Base at the largest shown budgets.|17}}

Self-generated rubrics ask an especially interesting question: can a policy improve the feedback it gives itself? The online self-rubric experiment shows encouraging MATH500 and Olympiad trends. On AIME24 and Minerva, however, it does not exceed Base at the largest displayed budgets. This is evidence of mixed practical benefit, not proof that self-feedback generally creates new reasoning abilities.

{{FIG:opd.png|1280|307|Figure 7. OPD with a Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct teacher. Low-budget gains coexist with lower high-budget coverage than Base on AIME24 and Minerva.|17}}

The OPD run offers a useful reference. Despite access to a larger teacher, its AIME24 and Minerva curves remain below Base at large k. A stronger teacher is therefore not, by itself, enough to guarantee a broader observed solution set in these runs.

There is also a practical distinction worth testing: text rubrics can be reused across rollouts and obtained from text-only APIs, whereas logit-based OPD requires access to teacher distributions. That suggests a potential cost advantage for rubrics, but these experiments do not yet measure it. Response-based distillation is another option when logits are unavailable.

My current view is that rubrics are a way to design supervision, not an automatic capability amplifier. Their value depends on whether the criteria produce reliable, discriminative feedback on the policy's own rollouts. A higher reward or a higher pass@1 is useful, but it answers a narrower question than expanding coverage.

07 / The experiments that would change my mind

  1. Measure gains and losses per problem. Compare Base-only, trained-only, shared, and unsolved sets under matched decoding and token budgets. Repeat sampling and report uncertainty; do not treat finite-sample failures as impossible problems.
  2. Inspect the reward signal inside each group. Log tie rates, reward dispersion, agreement with exact correctness, and rankings of partially correct solutions. Test whether rubric scores reward useful reasoning or merely presentation.
  3. Separate rubric quality from judge quality. Cross generator and judge models. Compare static, refreshed, and self-generated rubrics with identical scoring instructions, data, and training budgets.
  4. Run a clean reward ablation. Keep the rubric version fixed, normalize the score, vary its weight, and compare correctness-only, rubric-only, and hybrid rewards across seeds and checkpoints.
  5. Compare transfer and cost fairly. Match student compute and account for teacher tokens or API cost when comparing rubrics, distillation, and OPD. Evaluate on held-out tasks to check whether feedback transfers beyond the training criteria.
  6. Move to real agent tasks. Test tool-use decisions, execution constraints, and final delivery separately. Dynamic rubrics, a feedback buffer, curriculum learning, and comparisons with SDPO remain future experiments in these notes.
THE QUESTION I AM TAKING FORWARD

Can we make rubric feedback specific enough to guide learning, while preserving the rare solutions that broad exploration can still uncover?
